Shoulder dystocia occurs when the fetal anterior shoulder becomes stuck behind the maternal pubic symphysis after
the head delivers, preventing complete birth and creating a true obstetric emergency.
Key Assessment History: Large fetus (macrosomia), diabetes, prior shoulder dystocia.
Signs: Head delivers but retracts against perineum (“turtle sign”); body does not follow with next contraction, shoulders
impacted.
Field Management
1.Instruct Mother to Stop Pushing briefly to allow interventions.
2.Perform McRoberts Maneuver:
a.Hyperflex the mother’s legs tightly to her chest to rotate pelvis.
b.Most effective first-line maneuver in field.
3.Apply Suprapubic Pressure:
a.Apply firm downward pressure just above the pubic bone to dislodge shoulder (never fundal). Pressure should be directed
down and to the direction the fetus is facing.
b.Combine with McRoberts if needed.
4.Deliver Posterior Arm:
a.Gently insert a gloved hand and sweep posterior arm across the chest and out to reduce shoulder width.
5.Swift Action:
a.This is a time-sensitive emergency — prolonged dystocia can cause fetal hypoxia.
Do not transport until delivery is complete — baby must be delivered on scene if shoulder dystocia occurs.
If unable to deliver with posterior arm, repeat maneuvers starting with McRoberts. Change provider as needed.
Document all maneuvers used and duration of dystocia.
Do Not:
1.Apply fundal pressure (may worsen impaction).
2.Pull forcibly on the baby’s head or neck.
3.Delay maneuvers once shoulder dystocia is recognized.

tubing and pursue options for alternate access.

attempting to flush as resistance could indicate occlusion. Involve patient or family in
requesting normal access findings of port.

9. Inquire or observe the patient for burning, pain or discomfort at the portal site or
catheter track. If occlusion, displacement or other complication is suspected abandon
procedure immediately.

proper techniques to prevent inadvertent disconnection or displacement.

Indications
Acute Heart Failure, Cardiogenic Shock, Myocardial infarction, acute on
chronic ischemic cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, cardiac transplant
related graft failure, support doing PCI
Contraindications
Aortic regurgitation, severe aortic valve calcification, mechanical aortic
valves, aortic dissection, left ventricular thrombi, VSDs, severe sepsis,
severe PAD
Insertion 	Femoral or axillary artery
Complications
Anemia, hemolysis, arterial injury, aortic injury, aortic valve
insufficiency, arrhythmias, bleeding, cardiac tamponade, CVA, mitral
stenosis, limb ischemia, mitral regurgitation,
General Patient Management
1.Assess central and distal pulses.
2.Assess insertion site
3.Treat MAP
4.Clinical decision making based on arterial blood pressure must be based off of noninvasive
blood pressure art line versus the arterial pressure reading within the Impella, that is the
aorta pressure which is used for catheter placement.
5.Do not draw blood off of Impella art line.
6.Max HOB <30 Degrees
7.Ensure tubing (white pump cable and purge tubing) are clear of obstacles or kinking.
8.Keep AIC plugged in during transport if possible.
9.Never drop the P-level below P2.
10.Consider knee immobilizer for transport
11.Verify an ECHO has been completed prior to transport to verify placement.

First Pass
Definition
A first pass intubation refers to the successful placement of an
endotracheal tube into the trachea on the first attempt by a single
provider.
Key Elements:
"First attempt" means a single insertion of the laryngoscope blade into
the mouth with a maximum attempt time of <30 seconds without
adverse patient outcomes such as bradycardia or hypoxia
"Success" means verified correct tube placement in the trachea,
usually confirmed by waveform capnography, auscultation, and clinical
signs.
Documentation
Requirements
Clinical documentation requirements
“Airway protection secondary to impending respiratory failure” due to
altered mental status and concern for vomiting/aspiration, anticipated
clinical course (e.g., deteriorating neurologic status, impending arrest,
airway burns, facial trauma)
“Respiratory Failure” due to Hypoxia refractory to supplemental
oxygen, Inadequate ventilation (e.g., severe hypercapnia, respiratory
rate < 8)
“Intubation for uncontrolled agitation”
Example: The patient presented with a GCS of 7 and copious emesis, with
absent gag and cough reflexes. Due to altered mental status and concern
for vomiting/aspiration, as well as an anticipated clinical course including
deteriorating neurologic status, the patient required airway protection
secondary to impending respiratory failure. Endotracheal intubation was
performed to secure the airway prior to flight.
Example: The patient’s respiratory status deteriorated rapidly, with RR of 6
and increasing somnolence. SpO₂ dropped to 88% despite 15 L/min O₂.
Endotracheal intubation was required for respiratory failure due to
hypoxia refractory to supplemental oxygen and inadequate ventilation,
evidenced by severe hypercapnia and ineffective respiratory effort.
Example: The patient became increasingly combative and was
unresponsive to pharmacologic intervention. Continued agitation risked
airway compromise and injury during flight. For the safety of the patient
and crew, the patient required intubation for uncontrolled agitation.
